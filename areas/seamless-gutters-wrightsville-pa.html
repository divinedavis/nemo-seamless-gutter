Seamless Gutters in Wrightsville, PA

River exposure changes how a gutter has to be attached

The Susquehanna is wide where it passes Wrightsville, and there is nothing between the water and the borough to slow the wind down. Houses on River Road, on the streets that face the crossing to Columbia, and anything sitting up on the hillside above town all get gusts that hit the fascia and the underside of the gutter at the same time. That combination is what lifts gutters off a house — not rain weight.

So the fastening is the part I care about most on this side of the county. We use hidden hangers screwed through the trough into solid fascia, tightened up to roughly 16 inches on center on exposed elevations rather than the 24 inches that is fine on a sheltered interior wall. Old spike-and-ferrule systems, which are still on a lot of Wrightsville homes, work loose every time the wood swells and shrinks, and then wind finishes the job. Where the fascia is soft — common on the river-facing side of older frame houses — that gets addressed before anything new goes up, because a hanger is only as good as what it is screwed into.

Downspouts get the same attention. Straps every four to five feet, not just top and bottom, and an extra strap on tall gable runs where a loose spout will slap brick in a storm. On seamless gutter installation the trough itself is formed on site in one continuous piece, so there are no mid-run seams to work open when the whole assembly is flexing in a gust.

  • Hidden hangers tightened to about 16 inches on center on river-facing and hilltop elevations
  • Fascia checked and repaired before new gutter goes up, not after
  • Heavier-gauge aluminum where wind and ice load are both a factor
  • Sealed and riveted end caps and miters, since wind-driven rain finds any weak joint
  • Extra downspout straps on tall gable and stairwell runs
