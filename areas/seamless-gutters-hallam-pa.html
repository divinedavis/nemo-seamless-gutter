Post-war roof shapes and what they need

Most of Hallam went up in the decades after the Second World War, and you can read it from the street: single-story ranches with long, uninterrupted eaves, one-and-a-half story Capes with shed and gable dormers, and split-levels where an upper roof plane ends above a lower one. Each of those creates a different problem at the gutter line.

The ranches are the easy ones and the reason on-site forming matters. A forty or fifty foot eave run with no break should be one continuous length of gutter with no seams in the middle to work loose and weep. We roll seamless aluminum gutter on the truck in your driveway and cut it to the actual measurement of the house, so the only joints are at corners, end caps and outlets. That is the single biggest difference between a gutter that stays dry underneath and one that stains the fascia within a few years.

Capes and split-levels need more thought. Dormer valleys dump a large area of roof into a very short stretch of gutter, and that concentrated flow is what overshoots in a hard thunderstorm. In those spots we either step up to 6-inch gutter, tighten hanger spacing under the valley, or put an outlet close to where the water lands rather than at the far end of the run. On split-levels, an upper downspout that discharges onto the lower roof needs a splash guard and a lower gutter sized for the extra water — otherwise the lower run overflows every storm no matter how clean it is.

A lot of these houses also still carry their original gutter hung on spikes and ferrules. Spikes back out of aluminum fascia over thirty or forty winters, the gutter sags between them, water ponds, and the pitch is gone. Modern hidden hangers screwed into the fascia every two feet — closer than that where snow slides or a valley lands — hold the line straight and let us set a deliberate slope toward the outlets.

  • Long ranch eaves: one seamless run, no mid-span joints
  • Dormer and roof valleys: 6-inch gutter or an outlet placed at the point of impact
  • Split-level offsets: splash guard above, larger capacity below
  • Hidden hangers on screws instead of spike-and-ferrule on older fascia
