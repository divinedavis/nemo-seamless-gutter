5 vs 6 Inch Gutters: Right Size for York County Homes

Almost every homeowner who calls us about new gutters asks the same question within the first five minutes: should I go with 5-inch or 6-inch? The honest answer is that it depends less on your budget and more on your roof — how much water it collects, how fast that water arrives at the edge, and where it can leave. Here is how we actually work through that decision on York County homes.

The short version

Most single-family homes in York County are served well by 5-inch K-style seamless gutter with correctly sized and correctly placed downspouts. Six-inch becomes the better choice when the roof is large, steeply pitched, broken up by valleys that dump water into one spot, or when the home only has room for a limited number of downspouts. It is not a "nicer homes get 6-inch" situation — it is a math and drainage situation.

What the extra inch actually buys you

A 6-inch K-style profile carries meaningfully more water than a 5-inch — roughly 40 to 50 percent more cross-sectional area, depending on the exact profile. Just as important, 6-inch gutter is normally paired with 3" x 4" downspouts instead of 2" x 3". That downspout upgrade roughly doubles the outlet area, which is often the real bottleneck. Water backing over the front lip of a gutter during a thunderstorm is usually an outlet problem, not a trough problem.

Roof area and pitch

The number that matters is not your square footage of living space — it is the drainage area feeding each individual gutter run. A long rear roof plane on a two-story colonial in Dallastown or Manchester Township can send far more water to one gutter than the total footprint suggests. Pitch matters too: a steep roof moves water faster, so it hits the gutter with more velocity and less time to turn the corner into the downspout. Steep plus long usually points toward 6-inch.

Valleys and roof geometry

Complex rooflines with multiple gables and valleys concentrate water. If two large planes drain into a valley that terminates over ten feet of gutter, that section is doing the work of a much larger run. We look for those spots on the walk-around and size accordingly — sometimes with a wider gutter, sometimes with a splash guard and an added outlet.

How many downspouts you can realistically have

Rules of thumb are useful here: a 2" x 3" downspout handles a modest amount of roof area, and going beyond that means adding another outlet or moving to 3" x 4". If your home's layout, hardscaping, or landscaping limits where downspouts can go — a common issue on narrow city lots in York and Hanover — the wider gutter and bigger downspouts do the work that extra outlets otherwise would.

Trees and debris load

Under mature oaks, maples, and pines in older neighborhoods like Spring Garden Township, debris is the real enemy. A 6-inch trough clogs more slowly and holds more organic material before water starts spilling. If you are dealing with heavy leaf drop, talk to us about pairing the right size with gutter guards, and keep a realistic cleaning and repair schedule regardless of size.

Fascia, drip edge, and how it will look

This is the practical detail most homeowners never hear about. Six-inch gutter is taller and wider, so it needs adequate fascia to mount to. Homes with narrow fascia, low drip edge, or unusual trim details may need adjustments so the gutter sits correctly and water actually enters it instead of shooting over. Visually, 6-inch reads heavier — often a good match for larger homes and wide soffits, sometimes a mismatch on a small cape.

Points people get wrong

  • Bigger always means better performance. A 6-inch gutter with too few downspouts still overflows. Outlets and pitch matter as much as width.
  • Overflow means the gutter is undersized. Often it is a low spot, a clogged outlet, an ice dam, or a valley dumping past the gutter — fixable without upsizing.
  • You must use one size everywhere. Mixing is legitimate: 6-inch on the big rear run, 5-inch on a small porch or dormer.
  • Guards work the same in both sizes. Not quite — some guard styles perform better in wider troughs, and screen fit differs by profile.

A note on ice and winter here

York County winters bring freeze-thaw cycles that load gutters with ice. Neither size prevents ice damming — that is an attic insulation and ventilation issue. What helps is heavier-gauge aluminum, hidden hangers screwed into solid fascia at proper spacing, and downspouts that drain fully so they don't freeze solid.

How we decide on your house

We measure the actual runs, look at pitch and valley locations, count viable downspout locations, and check the fascia before recommending a size. Sometimes the answer is 5-inch with one more downspout instead of 6-inch.
