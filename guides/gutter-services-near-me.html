Gutter Services in York County, PA: What They Cost and What Actually Drives the Number

If you searched for gutter services near you, you are probably deciding between a repair, a cleaning, and a full replacement — here is what each one runs in York County and what changes the price.

The five services most York County homeowners are actually shopping for

"Gutter services" covers work that ranges from a two-hour service call to a full-day replacement of every run on the house. Sorting out which one you need is most of the pricing question, so start here.

The ranges below reflect what installed work generally goes for in York County and the surrounding area in 2026. They assume standard ladder access and a home in reasonable condition. Steep roofs, three stories, rotten fascia, or difficult access move every one of these numbers up.

  • Seamless aluminum gutter installation — roughly $9 to $14 per linear foot for 5-inch K-style, $12 to $18 for 6-inch. A typical York County house needing 150 to 200 feet lands somewhere around $1,800 to $3,500 in 5-inch, or $2,400 to $4,500 in 6-inch.
  • Downspouts — generally $10 to $16 per foot installed for aluminum, with 3x4 costing more than 2x3. Most homes need three to six of them.
  • Repairs — commonly $150 to $500 for resealing joints, re-pitching a sagging run, replacing hangers, or swapping a damaged section. Expect a minimum service charge on any small job; driving a truck and a crew to your house costs the same whether the fix takes 20 minutes or two hours.
  • Gutter cleaning — usually $150 to $350 for a single-story home and $250 to $500 for two stories, depending on roof pitch, tree load, and how long it has been.
  • Gutter guards — professionally installed micro-mesh and reverse-curve systems typically run $8 to $25 per foot, putting a whole-house job in the $1,500 to $4,500 range. Cheap snap-in screens cost far less and generally perform accordingly.

What moves your price up or down on a York County home

Two houses with the same footprint can quote thousands apart. These are the factors that explain the spread, and any honest estimator will walk them with you on site rather than quote off a satellite image.

  • Linear footage and number of corners. Corners, miters, and short runs take disproportionate labor. A simple ranch with four long runs is cheaper per foot than a home with dormers, bays, and multiple rooflines.
  • Gutter size and thickness. Six-inch gutter with 3x4 downspouts costs more up front but carries far more water — worth it on steep or large roof planes, and on the wooded lots common around Dover, Dillsburg, and the Susquehanna side of the county.
  • Material. Seamless aluminum is the practical default. Copper and half-round profiles, which suit some older homes in York City, Hanover, and Wrightsville, can run $30 to $50 per foot or more and are a different conversation entirely.
  • Fascia and soffit condition. This is the most common surprise. On older homes with wood fascia, freeze-thaw cycles and years of overflowing gutters rot the board the hangers screw into. Fascia repair or wrap typically adds several dollars per foot plus carpentry, and it has to be addressed before new gutter goes up or the new system will fail the same way.
  • Height and access. Two and three-story sections, roofs over decks or landscaping, and homes on the county's steeper lots take more setup time and more of it on ladders.
  • Drainage at the bottom. Extensions, splash blocks, and buried downspout lines are separate line items. Underground drainage typically runs $10 to $30 per foot of trench depending on soil and obstructions — and York County has plenty of shale and limestone that argues with a shovel.

Repair or replace: how to tell which service you need

Homeowners frequently pay for a replacement they did not need, or pay for three years of repairs on a system that was finished. The distinction is usually not the gutter itself — it is the pitch, the hangers, and the wood behind it.

Repair is the right call when the gutter body is straight and sound and the problem is localized: a leaking seam, a clogged downspout, one sagging span, a section torn off by a limb, or a mitered corner that opened up. That is a service call, not a project.

Replacement makes more sense when you see several of these at once: sectional gutter with seams leaking in multiple places, spikes and ferrules pulling out along an entire run, gutter that is pitched wrong from the day it went up, visible rot in the fascia behind the hangers, or gutter that is simply undersized for the roof it is catching. Once you are paying labor to open up and re-hang most of a run, the incremental cost of new seamless material is small, and you get a system without seams instead of a patched one.

  • Water staining or peeling paint on the fascia and soffit — the gutter has been overflowing or leaking behind itself.
  • Ice damming and heavy winter overflow — common in south-central Pennsylvania and often a capacity or pitch problem, not just weather.
  • Erosion trenches, mulch washed out of beds, or a damp basement wall on one side of the house — usually a downspout discharge problem that costs far less to fix than the water damage it causes.
  • Gutter that holds standing water after rain — wrong pitch, which shortens the life of everything it touches.

Cleaning and guards: where the money is well spent

Cleaning is maintenance, not a fix. In much of York County, oak and maple mean two cleanings a year — late spring after the seeds and blossoms drop, and late fall after the leaves are down. Homes with little overhang can often go to once a year. If you are paying for cleaning three or four times a year, guards start paying for themselves.

Guards are worth discussing honestly. A quality micro-mesh system genuinely reduces clogs and is a reasonable investment on a treed lot or a two-story home where climbing a ladder is a real hazard. No guard on the market eliminates maintenance completely — fine grit and shingle sand still need periodic rinsing, and mesh can hold a mat of pine needles that has to be brushed off. Any company that promises you will never touch your gutters again is selling you something.

Also worth knowing: guards installed over a gutter that is pitched wrong, undersized, or hanging off rotten fascia will not solve the problem you actually have. Fix the system first, then decide about guards.

How to compare local quotes — and how to get a real number for your house

The ranges on this page are for orientation. They are not a quote, and no contractor can give you a real one without seeing your fascia, measuring your runs, and looking at where the water is supposed to go. Anyone who quotes a firm price over the phone from a square-footage guess is either padding for the unknowns or planning to find them later as change orders.

When you get two or three estimates, put them side by side on the details rather than the bottom line. A cheaper number often means thinner aluminum, spike-and-ferrule hangers instead of hidden hangers, 2x3 downspouts where 3x4 belongs, or nothing at all budgeted for the rotted fascia the crew is going to find on install day.

  • Is the gutter seamless, formed on site, and what is the aluminum thickness?
  • What size gutter and downspout, and why that size for this roof?
  • What hanger system, and at what spacing?
  • Is fascia repair included, excluded, or priced as a contingency? Get this in writing.
  • Where does the water discharge, and is that included?
  • Is the company insured, and is the estimate itemized rather than a single lump figure?
  • Are old gutters removed and hauled away?
