Ice Dam Removal in York County, PA: Costs, Causes, and the Gutter Work That Follows

Ice dams in York County are an attic heat problem that ends up on your roof edge, and the cleanup usually splits into two bills: getting the ice off, and fixing what the ice tore loose.

What ice dam removal costs around York County

Emergency ice removal is priced by the hour or by the crew-hour, not by the foot, because nobody can see how thick the ice is until they are on the ladder. The ranges below reflect what south-central Pennsylvania homeowners are generally quoted in 2026 for the kind of winter we get here, where a thaw-freeze cycle in January or February can build a dam in forty-eight hours.

Low-pressure steam is the only method a reputable contractor should be using on a shingle roof. It melts a channel through the ice without stripping granules off the shingles. Pressure washers, hammers, chisels, and torches all cause damage that costs more than the ice dam did.

These are market ranges for context, not a quote from us. We are a seamless gutter contractor, so the honest scope is covered further down this page.

  • Steam removal, hourly: roughly $350 to $650 per hour in this region, often with a one or two hour minimum
  • Typical single ice dam, one or two roof edges: about $600 to $1,500 total
  • Large home, multiple valleys, or a dam that has been building for weeks: $2,000 to $4,000 and sometimes a second visit
  • After-hours, weekend, or storm-surge pricing: expect a premium during the week everyone calls at once
  • Roof raking the lower three or four feet of roof, as prevention between storms: roughly $150 to $400 per visit
  • Heat cable, professionally installed with a dedicated exterior outlet: commonly $15 to $30 per linear foot of roof edge and valley, so $800 to $2,500 on an average home

Why you have an ice dam, and why it is not really a gutter problem

An ice dam forms when heat escaping from the living space warms the roof deck, melts snow in the middle of the roof slope, and that meltwater runs down to the cold overhang and refreezes. The overhang is cold because there is no heated room underneath it. The ice ridge grows, water backs up behind it, and eventually it finds its way under the shingles and into the soffit, the fascia, or the ceiling.

That sequence happens whether gutters are present or not. Houses with no gutters at all still get ice dams. What gutters do is give the ice a place to anchor and a lot of extra weight to carry, which is why ice dam season is also gutter replacement season in York County.

If you keep getting dams in the same spot every winter, the fix is almost always upstream of the roofline: air sealing the attic floor, topping up insulation, clearing blocked soffit vents, or correcting a bath fan that dumps warm moist air into the attic instead of outside. Insulation and air sealing work in this area commonly runs from a few hundred dollars for targeted sealing up to several thousand for a full attic. It is unglamorous and it is the only thing that actually ends the cycle.

  • Warm spots on the roof deck: recessed lights, attic hatches, duct leaks, missing insulation over additions and cathedral ceilings
  • Blocked ventilation: soffit vents stuffed with blown insulation, no baffles, ridge vent painted or covered over
  • Low-slope sections and north-facing roofs that never see enough sun to clear
  • Complex rooflines: valleys, dormers, and porch roofs that collect drifted snow
  • Clogged gutters and downspouts, which do not cause the dam but make it heavier and hold water longer

While the ice is still up there

A few things are worth doing right now, and a few are worth avoiding. Most of the serious damage we see after a York County ice storm was not caused by the ice. It was caused by somebody on a ladder trying to get rid of it.

If water is already coming through a ceiling, put a container under it and poke a small drain hole at the lowest point of the bulge. A drywall patch costs far less than a ceiling that collapses because you tried to keep it intact.

  • Do pull snow off the lower part of the roof with a roof rake from the ground, before the next thaw
  • Do run a dehumidifier and check the attic for wet insulation after a leak, since that is where mold starts
  • Do photograph everything, including icicles and interior staining, before anything gets cleaned up
  • Do not chip, chisel, hammer, or pry at the ice, which takes shingles and gutter with it
  • Do not put a ladder against a gutter loaded with ice, because the gutter is already carrying more weight than it was built for
  • Do not use rock salt or a torch on the roof; calcium chloride in a stocking is the less damaging stopgap, and it is still only a stopgap
  • Do not climb an icy roof at all, for any reason
